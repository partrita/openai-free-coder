# CCR8: a promising therapeutic target against tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells

Yuanjia Wen1,2,4 ∙ Yu Xia1,2,4 ∙ Xiangping Yang3 ∙ Huayi Li1,2 Send email to huayilee@foxmail.com ∙ Qinglei Gao1,2 Send email to qingleigao@hotmail.com

## Significance
Current cancer therapeutics targeting tumor-infiltrating regulatory T (TI-Treg) cells exhibit modest efficacy and harbor risks of systemic toxicities in mice and humans. The molecule CC chemokine receptor (CCR)8 is predominantly expressed on TI-effector Treg cells, the principal subset of Treg cells within tumor microenvironments (TMEs) that can promote immunosuppression and tumor progression. An improved understanding of CCR8 functions and advancing research assessing anti-CCR8 agents may be promising in the development of Treg cell-focused putative therapeutics for certain cancers, ideally leading to strong efficacy and diminished immune-related adverse events (irAEs).

## Highlights

Regulatory T (Treg) cells play key roles in maintaining immune homeostasis in normal tissues while facilitating immune evasion within tumor microenvironments (TMEs).
The anticipated success of current strategies targeting tumor infiltrating (TI)-Treg cells has not been materialized in clinical practice, partially due to severe immune-related adverse events.
CC chemokine receptor (CCR)8 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target against TI-Treg cells for its predominant expression on effector TI-Treg cells and minimal expression on peripheral Treg and conventional T cells in mice and humans.
Anti-CCR8 agents have shown encouraging antitumor activity and safety in preclinical models and Phase 1/2 clinical trials for various cancers, including gastric cancer, representing a rising class of next-generation putative immunotherapies.
Abstract
Tumor-infiltrating regulatory T (TI-Treg) cells constitute key components within the tumor microenvironment (TME) to suppress antitumor immunity and facilitate tumor progression. Although multiple therapies have been developed to eliminate TI-Treg cells, most of them exhibit only modest efficacy and harbor risks of inducing immune-related adverse events (irAEs). Recent studies demonstrate that CC chemokine receptor (CCR)8 is highly and specifically expressed on effector TI-Treg cells in mice and humans, highlighting CCR8 as a promising target for selective TI-Treg cell depletion in the treatment of various cancers. Here, we concentrate on the latest understanding of CCR8 regarding its expression, functions, and regulation, and summarize the current landscape of CCR8-targeted therapies. With favorable efficacy and safety, the latter represent an important class of next-generation putative cancer immunotherapies.

Treg-cell-targeted cancer therapeutics: from past to future
CD4+ regulatory T (Treg) cells (see Glossary) play pivotal, widespread roles in maintaining physiologic homeostasis and regulating the pathogenesis of various pathologies, including autoimmune or inflammatory diseases, as well as cancers [1–3]. Within the TME, TI-Treg cells facilitate immune evasion, fuel tumor progression, and are associated with the unfavorable outcomes of most cancers [4]. Multiple therapies have been developed to eliminate TI-Treg cells, aiming to alleviate immunosuppression and enhance antitumor immune responses. However, the anticipated success of those therapies has not materialized, due to the modest efficacy and concerning risks of irAEs [5].
Recent studies show that CCR8 is a well-recognized marker of activation for TI-Treg cells in mice and humans, and is possibly responsible for Treg cell functions, differentiation, migration, and survival [6–8]. CCR8 is preferentially expressed on TI-Treg cells, especially those exhibiting highly immunosuppressive properties [9,10]. This unique expression profile of CCR8 offers possible opportunities for precisely depleting TI-Treg cells. A range of anti-CCR8 agents, including monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), antagonists, and bispecific antibodies, have been developed and demonstrate encouraging antitumor activity without obvious severe irAEs in preclinical models and Phase 1/2 clinical trials across various cancer types, including gastric cancer [10,11].
In this review, we highlight the potential of CCR8 as an ideal target for TI-Treg cell depletion by delineating its dominant expression, biological functions, and signaling pathways in TI-Treg cells. We comprehensively summarize the latest advances in the development of anti-CCR8 agents in preclinical models and clinical trials. Therapies targeting CCR8 may offer superior efficacy and reduced irAEs due to the relative specificity for TI-Treg cells compared with previous targets, and hold promise for overcoming the limitations of certain current Treg-cell-targeted cancer therapeutics.
Challenges of current cancer therapeutics targeting TI-Treg cells
CD4+ T cells that exhibit high expression of CD25 and FOXP3 are widely recognized as Treg cells. Treg cells are extensively distributed across human tissues such as the bone marrow [12], gut [13], skin [14], central nervous system [15], muscle [16], and lungs [17], where they play pivotal roles in maintaining immune homeostasis. By contrast, TI-Treg cells, which are thought to be primarily recruited from the Treg cell pool in peripheral blood [18], are key contributors to the suppression of antitumor immunity. Increased amounts of TI-Treg cells are associated with tumor metastasis, disease progression, and poor prognosis in most cancers [19]. Notably, substantial differences in Treg cell infiltration are well-documented across different cancers. In fact, even within the same tumor type, TI-Treg cell abundance varies among individuals, which is often associated with specific genetic alterations (Box 1).

Effects of genetic alterations on TI-Treg cell infiltration
Oncogene mutations can alter downstream signaling pathways, thereby reprogramming factors that facilitate Treg cell infiltration. In EGFR-mutated lung adenocarcinomas, CD4+ effector Treg cells have exhibited high infiltration into the TME due to enhanced CCR4-CCL22 signaling via activated cJun N-terminal kinase, which was reversed by EGFR inhibitors [69]. In gastric cancer, genomic analysis identified a correlation between high Treg cell infiltration and RHOA mutations, which activated the downstream PI3K-AKT-mTOR signaling cascade and increased free fatty acid production. Because Treg cells were more efficient utilizing free fatty acids as energy sources than effector T (Teff) cells, they predominated in the TME of patients with RHOA mutations [70]. Moreover, the expression of BRAFV600E in an inducible autochthonous mouse model of melanoma upregulated Ccr4 cognate chemokines (Ccl2, Ccl17, and Ccl22), leading to Treg cell recruitment at sites of nascent tumors in the skin. Treatment with a BRAF inhibitor reduced the expression of these chemokines and the migration of Treg cells to the skin [71].
Beyond inducing Treg cell recruitment, genetic alterations can stimulate the secretion of cytokines that reprogram CD4+ Tconv cells into Treg cells. In patients with non-small cell lung cancer, especially lung squamous carcinomas (LUSC), mutations in the PTEN/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway or PTEN loss correlated with higher abundance of TI-Treg cells and poor responses to immunotherapy [72]. Using a Pten-deficient LUSC-bearing mouse model, researchers showed that PTEN loss upregulated TGF-β and CXCL10 to facilitate the conversion of Tconv cells to Treg cells [72]. Mutations in the KRAS oncogene also induced the conversion to Treg cells by promoting the secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β via activation of the MEK-ERK-AP1 pathway – an effect that was reversed by inhibiting KRAS expression [73]. In addition, highly immunosuppressive ICOShi/PD-1neg Treg cells were amplified in secondary acute myeloid leukemia patients with TP53 mutations [74], although the underlying mechanisms remain enigmatic.
In our recent work, we confirmed a remarkably higher abundance of eTreg cells in HGSOC tumors with HRD compared with homologous recombination‐proficient (HRP) tumors [40]. Specifically, genomic instability that was driven by HRD in an interferon (IFN) signaling-enriched TME, upregulated MHC-II transcription and induced the Treg cell phenotype via aberrant stimulation of TCR signaling; this translated into a deficiency in essential co-stimulatory signals and elevated co-inhibitory signals [40].
In most cancers, effector Treg (eTreg) cells that impair antitumor immune responses constitute the main subset of TI-Treg cells, accounting for up to 50% of all CD4+ T cells within the TME [4]. Of note, compared with Treg cells in circulation, a range of surface molecules related to T cell activation are upregulated in TI-Treg cells characterized by co-stimulatory molecules (CD27, ICOS, OX40, 4-1BB, and GITR), co-inhibitory molecules (CTLA-4, PD-1, LAG3, and TIGIT), and chemokine receptors (CCR4 and CCR8) [20–22]. These upregulated markers provide actionable therapeutic targets for the elimination or attenuation of Treg-cell-mediated immunosuppression.
Multiple strategies have been developed to deplete Treg cells for cancer treatment. These approaches include targeting surface molecules or intracellular signaling pathways in Treg cells, blocking Treg cell recruitment, modulating Treg cell metabolism, and eliminating Treg-cell-derived factors within the TME [5,23–25]. While these strategies have shown antitumor potential, their clinical application is restricted by the risk of irAEs due to on-target/off-tumor toxicities. These toxicities are currently inevitable because the targeted molecules are not uniquely expressed by TI-Treg cells and also exist on peripheral Treg cells and antitumor effector T cells [26]. An ideal strategy for targeting Treg cells for cancer therapy is to selectively deplete eTreg cells infiltrated into the TME while preserving CD4+ conventional T (Tconv) cells and peripheral Treg cells, thereby minimizing unwanted toxicities. Achieving this goal requires in part, the identification of specific markers for TI-eTreg cells.
Predominant expression of CCR8 on TI-eTreg cells
Human CCR8 was first identified in monocytes and thymus in 1997 [27], and was later shown to be expressed on CD4+ T helper (Th)2 cells [28,29] (Figure 1). This discovery shifted CCR8-related research towards type 2 inflammatory diseases in the following decade. Besides monocytes and T lymphocytes, CCR8 was detected on natural killer (NK) cells within human dermal cell suspensions [30], with its expression being upregulated upon interleukin (IL)-2 treatment in vitro [31,32]. Additionally, CCR8 was identified on in vitro cultured human vascular smooth muscle cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells, where it facilitated CC chemokine ligand (CCL)1-mediated chemotaxis [33,34]. Moreover, flow cytometric analysis of monocyte-derived dendritic cells (DCs) derived from human peripheral blood showed expression of CCR8 [35].

Recently, single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis of human tumor samples demonstrated that CCR8 expression was specifically upregulated in TI-Treg cells within human breast, colorectal, and non-small cell lung cancers, sparking interest in CCR8 as a potential therapeutic target in oncology [9,22]. Growing evidence has highlighted the unique expression profile of CCR8 on TI-Treg cells across various cancer types, in contrast to Treg cells derived from normal tissues [9,10,22,36–38]. In mice and humans, CCR8 is expressed by up to 80% of tumor-infiltrating CD4+ FOXP3+ T cells in cancers such as kidney, bladder, and colorectal cancers, as well as in melanoma [39]. By comparison, CCR8+ Treg cells represent less than 10% of Treg cells in other tissues such as the lymph nodes [39]. Although the frequency of CCR8+ cells varies between cancer types, CCR8 expression remains consistently higher in Treg cells, especially in eTreg cells, than in Tconv cells [6,7,9,10]. In our recent study, we captured over 20 000 Treg cells from human high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) samples using scRNA-seq and classified them into ten distinct subgroups, each exhibiting unique marker expression profiles; CCR8 was predominantly enriched in terminal eTreg cells [40]. Upon activation by tumor-associated antigens, eTreg cells would clonally expand and exert immunosuppressive effects. In the CT26 tumor-bearing mouse model, single-cell analysis of T cell receptor (TCR) clonotypes and gene expression profiles showed that the most distinguishing feature of clonally expanding Treg cells compared with nonexpanding ones, was the expression of CCR8 [39].
Multifarious CCR8 roles
Human CCR8 is a chemokine receptor with four ligands including CCL1, CCL8, CCL16, and CCL18 [8,27,41]. Whether CCR8 affects the immunosuppressive functions, migration, and survival of Treg cells remains controversial. A recent study utilized a novel cellular model simulating TI-Treg cells in vitro and showed that CCR8 did not regulate the immunosuppressive properties, proliferation, or survival of Treg cells [42]. In the Ccr8−/− tumor mouse model bearing MC38 (adenocarcinoma), the loss of CCR8 did not alter the frequency or quantity of Treg cells, nor did it promote the function of CD4+ Tconv cells or CD8+ T cells [43]. No significant differences in tumor growth curves were observed between Ccr8−/− and Ccr8+/+ mice in either MC38 or B16-F10-melanoma tumor models [43]. These findings were consistent with the data derived from mAbs targeting CCR8 [10]. In both Lewis lung carcinoma cell line (LLC)-ovalbumin (OVA) and MC38 mouse models, the lack of CCR8 expression or the use of a CCR8-specific nanobody that only blocked CCR8 signaling did not affect the recruitment, activation, or functional properties of Treg cells, nor did it impact tumor growth [10]. By contrast, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC)-prone CCR8 blockade resulted in NK cell-mediated depletion of CCR8+ TI-Treg cells without functionally affecting CD4+ Tconv cells or periphery Treg cells in other organs such as the tumor-draining lymph nodes and spleen [10]. Therefore, the targeting mechanism of CCR8 therapeutic antibodies should encompass not only functional blockade but also the depletion of CCR8+ TI-Treg cells via ADCC to enhance antitumor immune responses.
Contradictory evidence exists regarding the roles of CCR8 and its ligands in regulating Treg cell migration, functions, survival, and differentiation (Figure 2, Key Figure). The in vitro coculture assay of CD4+ Treg cells with chemokines CCL1 and/or CCL18 showed that the chemotactic index of CD4+ CCR8+ Treg cells increased with rising chemokine concentrations, exceeding that of CD4+ CCR8- Treg cells, thus indicating the key role of CCR8 in mediating Treg cell migration [7]. Moreover, an earlier in vitro study demonstrated that CCL1 neutralization impaired the suppressive effect of Treg cells on CD8+ T cell proliferation [44]. Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data from patients with muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) showed that the highest gene enriched pathway in tumors bearing high CCR8 expression, involved the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, compared with tumors with low CCR8 expression [6]. As the main ligand of CCR8, CCL1 induced STAT3 phosphorylation and enhanced Treg-cell-mediated immunosuppression in a STAT3-dependent manner; this was validated using bioinformatic analysis of the TCGA-MIBC cohort, as well as flow cytometric analysis of fresh human MIBC samples, in addition to intervention experiments in in vitro cultured human and murine Treg cells [6,8].

Specifically, CCR8 upregulated the expression of transcription factors FOXO1 and c-MAF, downstream of STAT3, and induced the expression of effector molecules such as CD39, IL-10, and granzyme B [6,8]. Importantly, in vitro cultured CCR8– Treg cells were more prone to apoptosis than wild-type Treg cells due to the CCR8-mediated promotion of Treg cell survival through its physical interactions with DCs, indicating the significance of CCR8 for Treg cell survival [45]. Fibroblast-derived CCL1 promoted the differentiation of splenic T cells into FOXP3+ Treg cells through the CCL1-CCR8 axis, leading to a remarkable increase in Treg cell numbers and the formation of an immunologically tolerant premetastatic niche in the LLC-exo mouse model [46]. Accordingly, treatment with a CCR8 antagonist AZ084 effectively restrained Treg cell differentiation and accumulation in the lungs, thereby retarding tumor metastasis [46].
Collectively, the complexity of the TME, bearing heterogeneous cell populations with varying cytokine concentrations and a multitude of yet-unexplored factors, likely contributes to the opposing observations on the roles of CCR8 in Treg cells across different models. The potential of CCR8 to modulate Treg cell function under specific conditions warrants further investigation in a broader range of conditional knockout mouse models and ideally, in human clinical trials. Leveraging cutting-edge technologies such as single-cell analysis and spatial-omics might provide deeper insights into the intricate interaction between the TME and the impact of CCR8 on Treg cell functions.
The effects of CCR8 on other cells have also been reported. In tumor cells, CCR8 mediated antiapoptotic effects were confirmed to occur via the ERK-MAPK signaling cascade in vitro in cultured BW5147 cells [47]. This effect may be an underlying mechanism of tumor resistance to apoptosis in adult T cell leukemia (ATL), because inhibition of CCR8 signaling using pertussis toxin increased the susceptibility of ATL cells to undergo apoptosis, both with and without external apoptotic stimuli [48]. Similarly, the administration of a CCR8 agonist rescued murine thymic lymphoma cells and murine thymocytes from dexamethasone-induced apoptosis in vitro [49]. In addition, CCR8 facilitated the conversion of CD4+ Tconv cells to Treg cells in vitro in a transforming growth factor (TGF)-β-dependent manner; this was inhibited via blockade of CCR8 signaling using anti-CCL1 mAbs [44]. Moreover, CCR8 on vascular endothelial cells promoted angiogenesis in assays using models of rabbit cornea and the chick chorioallantoic membrane [50]. Therefore, CCR8 can act on various cell types.
Regulation of CCR8 expression
CCR8 expression on Treg cells is regulated by a variety of factors. In the experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE) mouse model of multiple sclerosis, CCL1 secreted by Treg cells upregulates CCR8 expression on Treg cells, as part of a positive feedback mechanism [8]. CCR8 expression is driven by the two transcription factors GATA3 and IRF4, which are related to Th2 cell development and facilitate preferential access of Treg cells to type 2 inflammatory sites in mice and humans [51–53]. scRNA-seq analysis in the LLC-OVA mouse model indicated that TCR-mediated NF-κB signaling induced Ccr8 expression [10]. Furthermore, when cocultured with tumor tissue culture supernatant (TTCS), Treg cells isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of colorectal cancer patients upregulated their CCR8 expression responding to tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α within the TTCS through the TNFR2/NF-κB signaling pathway [54]. Though current evidence suggests that CCR8 is predominantly expressed on TI-Treg cells, it remains unclear whether specific regulators within the TME can upregulate CCR8 expression on cells other than Treg cells, warranting further investigation.
The promising advances of anti-CCR8 agents
As research on CCR8 advances, the molecular mechanisms and structure framework underlying CCL1-CCR8 interactions and CCR8 inhibition by antibodies and nonpeptide ligands have been progressively elucidated [55,56]. In-depth structural profiling provides novel insights into the development of antibodies and nonpeptide agonists targeting CCR8.
Preclinical models
The higher expression of CCR8 in TI-eTreg cells suggests that anti-CCR8 agents are used to effectively target TI-Treg cells while minimally affecting Tconv and peripheral Treg cells. As discussed later, emerging evidence underscores the promising antitumor efficacy of anti-CCR8 agents across various preclinical models. Anti-CCR8 agents were shown to deplete eTreg cells, mitigate the exhaustion of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), promote the development of tumor-specific memory Tconv cells, activate DCs, and elicit less autoimmune responses compared with other strategies targeting Treg cells (Table 1).

In the Foxp3EGFP-DTR mouse tumor model of B16-F10 melanoma, depletion of CD4+ FOXP3+ Treg cells with diphtheria toxin (DTx) led to the activation and substantial expansion of a subset of CD4+ FOXP3– Tconv cells, characterized by CCR8 expression. These cells acquired a transcriptional profile similar to Treg cells, and also exhibited immunosuppressive capabilities [57]. This finding provided an additional rationale for targeting CCR8, because anti-CCR8 agents not only selectively eliminated TI-eTreg cells but also depleted newly generated Treg-cell-like immunosuppressive Tconv cells [57].
The synergistic potential of combining anti-CCR8 agents with other cancer therapies is a noteworthy consideration. The TME of immunologically cold tumors is characterized by a high abundance of Treg cells, which contribute to the resistance to anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint mAbs [58,59]. Accordingly, anti-CCR8 mAbs might deplete Treg cells and turn cold tumors into hot ones, thereby enhancing the sensitivity to anti-PD-1 mAbs. Anti-CCR8 mAb therapy was shown to promote the expansion of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells and induce durable memory responses in the CT26 tumor-bearing colorectal carcinoma mouse model, significantly improving the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 blockade [39]. Approximately 15% of cancer patients treated with anti-PD-1 mAbs experience accelerated tumor growth, a phenomenon known as hyper-progressive disease (HPD), which has been attributed in part to enhanced Treg cell activity and an imbalance in the ratios of Treg and Tconv cells [60,61]. Thus, we posit that concomitant administration of anti-CCR8 drugs to impair Treg cells might reduce HPD. Administering a combination of anti-PD-1 and anti-CCR8 mAbs has demonstrated synergistic reduction in tumor growth without evident autoimmune adverse effects in mouse models of various cancers [10]. We conducted a Phase 2, single-arm, open label, multi-center, interventional study (NCT04507841)i in patients (n = 67) with homologous recombination deficiency (HRD)-positive HGSOC treated with neoadjuvant poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor monotherapy (niraparib) [primary outcomes: the percentage of patients receiving R0 resection after treatment, and overall response rate (ORR) after treatment]. We observed that the proportion of post-treatment TI-eTreg cells positively correlated with post-treatment tumor burden, as reflected by serum cancer antigen 125 (CA125) concentrations in the treatment group [40]. In genetically modified HRD-positive ID8 or E0771 tumor-bearing mouse models, the combination of a PARP inhibitor and an anti-CCR8 mAb significantly suppressed tumor progression compared with either agent alone, largely attributed to TI-eTreg cell depletion [40]. In addition, analysis of single-cell transcriptomes upon Treg cell depletion mediated by DTx in the KrasLSL-G12D/WTTrp53fl/flFoxp3GFP-DTR adenocarcinoma mouse model showed remarkable upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling-related genes in endothelial cell subsets. This profile partially counteracted the inhibitory effects of Treg cell elimination on tumor progression, suggesting a potential rebound mechanism [62]. Accordingly, the combination of VEGF blockade with anti-CCR8 agents to deplete Treg cells improved antitumor efficacies compared with monotherapy without obvious adverse events; this suggested a novel combination treatment strategy for these solid tumors [62].
In addition to anti-CCR8 mAbs, two anti-CCR8/CTLA-4 bispecific antibodies, GBD201 and 2MW4691, have been developed [63,64]. Compared with anti-CCR8 mAbs, the CCR8-targeting arm of GBD201 demonstrates full blocking activity and robust binding properties, with best-in-class potential. Its binding affinity for CTLA-4 is intentionally reduced to minimize severe irAEs commonly associated with CTLA-4 inhibition. GBD201 not only induced Treg cell depletion through enhanced ADCC, but also inhibited Treg cell migration by blocking CCL1–CCR8 signaling in in vivo studies using hCCR8/hCTLA-4 double knockin mice. Compared with anti-CCR8 mAbs or anti-CTLA-4 mAbs, GBD201 exhibited comparable or superior in vivo tumor growth suppression [63]. Similarly, 2MW4691 led to in vitro ADCC in Treg cells with high expression of CTLA-4 and CCR8 but had no effect on CD8+ T cells with low CTLA-4 expression [64]. The in vivo efficacy and safety profile of 2MW4691 were further verified in models of MC38 tumor-bearing mice and cynomolgus monkeys [64].
Clinical trials
Since it remains unclear whether CCR8 is indispensable for Treg cell immunosuppression, it is crucial to assess inhibiting CCL1-CCR8 interactions while concurrently depleting TI-Treg cells through ADCC and/or antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP). To this end, the majority of anti-CCR8 therapies in development rely on nonfucosylated humanized IgG1 mAbs that lead to enhanced ADCC activity due to the absence of core fucose in the Fc N-glycan. This modification increases the binding affinity of IgG1 Fc to the FcgRIIIα receptor on effector immune cells, including NK cells [65]. Several anti-CCR8 mAbs have entered Phase 1/2 clinical trials to assess the safety, recommended dosing, and efficacy of these mAbs, either as monotherapies, or in combination with other therapeutics (Table 2).

Most clinical trials are still in the process of recruiting patients with few data reported. A Phase 1, dose-escalation trial of SRF114 (an anti-CRR8 mAb) (NCT05635643)ii recently reported its preliminary results of safety and robust effects of CCR8+ Treg cell depletion in 15 patients with advanced solid tumors [66]. In addition, an aggregate analysis of results from three first-in-human, dose-escalation Phase1/2 trials of an anti-CCR8 mAb (LM-108) (NCT05199753iii, NCT05255484iv, and NCT05518045v) reported encouraging antitumor activity of LM-108 combined with anti-PD-1 mAbs in 36 patients with gastric cancer [ORR: 36.1%; disease control rate (DCR): 72.2%; median progression-free survival: 6.53 months], especially in 11 patients with resistance to anti-PD-1 mAb therapy (ORR: 63.6%; DCR: 81.8%) [11]. These results support the extended investigation of anti-CCR8 mAbs in larger-scale trials.
Although a list of small molecules has been identified as CCR8 antagonists, only a limited number have progressed to clinical evaluations. IPG7236, a potent and selective CCR8 antagonist, is the first small molecule to be evaluated in Phase 1/2a, multicenter, dose-escalation clinical trials (NCT05142592vi and NCT05288543vii) to assess safety and tolerability in patients with advanced solid tumors and healthy adults. It has been reported that IPG7236 exerts its antitumor effects via Treg cell depletion and CD8+ CTLs quantity boosting; both IPG7236 monotherapy and its combination with PD-1 blockade significantly controlled tumor progression in a mouse xenograft model of human breast cancer MDA-MB-231 cells [67].
Concluding remarks
Current knowledge highlights the significant immunosuppressive roles of TI-Treg cells, positioning TI-Treg cell depletion as a promising avenue to next-generation immunotherapies. However, despite considerable efforts made to reduce Treg cell numbers and reverse their immunosuppression, therapeutic molecules in development targeting Treg cells such as CTLA-4 and CD25 have not demonstrated sufficient antitumor efficacy and safety profile. Anti-CCR8 agents have displayed promising potential for antitumor efficacy while minimizing on-target/off-tumor toxicities. Although knowledge on CCR8 and Treg cell biology has progressed in recent years, significant challenges and limitations remain (see Outstanding questions), and further studies are needed to elucidate the roles of CCR8 within the TME to fully harness the potential of anti-CCR8 agents. Targeting CCR8 as an antitumor therapy is not without possible side effects. Human skin might also be a reservoir of CCR8+ cells [68]. Consequently, patients treated with anti-CCR8 therapies may experience skin-related irAEs, which require special care and close monitoring in clinical trials. Additionally, anti-CCR8 treatments also eliminate CCR8+ Treg cells in the inflamed afflicted tissues in mouse models of EAE and autoimmune arthritis, potentially exacerbating ongoing autoimmune diseases [39]. Therefore, extra care should be taken to exclude such populations when designing or participating in clinical trials. Moreover, the varying expression of CCR8 across tumor types, coupled with the impact of multifarious factors within the TME regarding CCR8 expression, underscores the need for thorough assessments to evaluate the potentially differential efficacy of anti-CCR8 therapies in different cancers. Clinical evaluations of anti-CCR8 agents are still in nascent stages, warranting further assessments in larger clinical trials. Nevertheless, it is encouraging that CCR8 has emerged as a promising therapeutic target against TI-Treg cells in solid tumors, and anti-CCR8 agents represent one important family of next-generation putative cancer immunotherapies.
Outstanding questions
Why is CCR8 specifically upregulated in a specific population of TI-Treg cells with strong immunosuppressive activity? Despite multiple lines of evidence indicating the close correlation between TI-Treg cells and high CCR8 expression, the underlying mechanisms remain obscure.
Is CCR8 indispensable for the migration, survival, differentiation, and immunosuppressive functions of Treg cells? The roles of CCR8 in Treg cells remain controversial, and requires further and rigorous validation, including utilizing conditional knockout mouse models. Resolving this ambiguity can inform the design and development of anti-CCR8 agents as putative cancer treatments.
What are the essential roles of CCR8 in cells other than TI-Treg cells? CCR8 is expressed by a small fraction of cells other than TI-Treg cells, raising concerns of potential on-target off-tumor toxicities. Further investigations into the functions of CCR8 in these cells are required to fully assess the safety profile of anti-CCR8 therapies.
Can CCR8-focused cancer therapeutics acquire approvals in clinical practice? Although clinical trials of anti-CCR8 agents are rapidly advancing, most are currently in early-phase stages, with ongoing patient recruitment and few available efficacy or safety data. The path to clinical approval remains lengthy.
Is there a difference in the efficacy of anti-CCR8 therapies across different cancer types? It is reasonable to speculate that tumors with high infiltration of Treg cells, especially effector Treg cells expressing CCR8, might be more sensitive to anti-CCR8 therapies. The intended populations for anti-CCR8 therapies remain to be verified in clinical trials.

Glossary
Antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC)
cells with killing activity (e.g., NK cells, macrophages, neutrophils, etc.) that recognize Fc segments of antibodies binding to target antigens (e.g., bacteria or tumor cells) through Fc receptors (FcR) to directly kill target cells.
Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP)
upon specific recognition and binding of antigenic epitopes by antibodies, Fc segments can bind to FcRs on the surface of macrophages, prompting macrophage phagocytosis of target cells.
CC chemokine receptor type 8 (CCR8)
type of G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR).
Co-inhibitory molecules
Cell surface molecules and their ligands that provide immunosuppressive signals and act to downregulate or terminate T (or B) cell activation.
Conventional T (Tconv) cells
T lymphocytes that express an αβ T cell receptor, as well as a co-receptor CD4 or CD8.
Co-stimulatory molecules
Cell surface molecules and their ligands that provide co-stimulatory signals for full T (or B) cell activation.
Immune homeostasis
A state in which the immune system is tolerant to its own components while responding moderately to non-self-antigenic stimuli.
Immune-related adverse events (irAEs)
adverse drug reactions at all levels thought to be related to immune mechanisms from antineoplastic drugs/treatments (e.g., in clinical trials).
Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor
type of small molecule drug that inhibits the PARP family of DNA repair enzymes; it effectively kills tumor cells through DNA repair inhibition and synergistic lethal effects and has been widely used in the treatment of various cancers.
Regulatory T (Treg) cells
subgroup of T cells characterized by the CD4, CD25, and FOXP3 expression phenotype; prominent in maintaining immune tolerance and inhibiting antitumor immunity.
